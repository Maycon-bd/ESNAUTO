RESEARCH ARTICLE
Enhanced Multistep Prediction of Multivariate Market
Indices Using Weighted Optical Reservoir Computing
Fang Wang 1 · Ting Bu 2 · Yuping Huang 1,2
Received: 14 February 2025 / Revised: 10 June 2025 / Accepted: 20 June 2025
© The Author(s) 2025
Abstract
We propose and experimentally demonstrate an innovative weighted optical reservoir computing system for market
index prediction. By integrating fundamental market data with macroeconomic indicators and technical metrics,
we capture the broader dynamics of the stock market. Our system shows signiﬁcantly higher performance than
the state-of-the-art methods such as linear regression, decision trees, and neural network architectures including
long short-term memory. It effectively captures the market’s high volatility and nonlinear behaviors under limited
data conditions, demonstrating strong potential for real-time, parallel, multi-dimensional data processing, and
prediction.
Keywords Optical reservoir computing · Parallel data processing · Multivariate forecasting · Multistep prediction
1 Introduction
Over the past few decades, machine learning (ML) has emerged as a transformative tool in ﬁnancial market
prediction, particularly for forecasting market indices [ 1]. The core challenge in this ﬁeld lies in effectively
integrating diverse information sources and building models capable of capturing the inherent complexity of
ﬁnancial markets. A wide range of ML techniques-including regression, classiﬁcation, deep learning, and ensemble
approaches-have been applied to forecast stock and market trends [ 2–9]. Among them, long-short-term memory
(LSTM) networks, a specialized class of recurrent neural networks (RNNs), have gained prominence for their
ability to capture temporal dependencies and nonlinear patterns in sequential data [ 10–16]. Leveraging historical
prices, technical indicators [ 17], and macroeconomic variables, these ML models can generate nuanced forecasts
and provide insights into market behavior.
Despite their promise, existing ML methods for market prediction face several limitations. Most require large
volumes of dense historical data for training, which can be resource-intensive and not always practical. Moreover,
many models struggle to capture sudden market shifts or peaks, often leading to unreliable predictions under volatile
conditions. These limitations hinder their effectiveness for real-time forecasting and ﬁnancial decision-making.
RNNs have long served as foundational architectures for modeling temporal dynamics, but their training is often
challenged by issues such as vanishing and exploding gradients. To address these issues, alternative frameworks
have been developed. One such approach is Backpropagation-Decorrelation (BPDC), an efﬁcient online learning
algorithm introduced by Steil [ 18], which reduces computational complexity to O(N) while enhancing training
stability. Another inﬂuential direction is Reservoir Computing (RC), which simpliﬁes learning by keeping the
recurrent component-known as the reservoir-ﬁxed and training only the output layer [ 19]. A prominent RC model
is the Echo State Network (ESN), proposed by Jaeger [ 20], which employs a randomly initialized reservoir that

Recent studies have demonstrated that RC architectures can perform competitively on time-series tasks, even
with limited training data [ 21–23]. This makes them particularly attractive for ﬁnancial applications where low-
latency, real-time, and data-efﬁcient prediction is critical. An RC system consists of a dynamic reservoir that
projects input into a high-dimensional space, and a readout layer that extracts patterns from the resulting reservoir
states [24]. As only the readout is trained-typically with simple linear regression-RC allows for fast training and
efﬁcient computation [25]. Furthermore, the ﬁxed nature of the reservoir makes RC highly suitable for hardware
implementation. In particular, optical reservoir computing has attracted attention for its ability to leverage photonic
hardware for ultra-fast and parallel computation [ 23, 26–28]. Optical RC systems can be categorized into free-
space optical RC, ﬁber-based temporal RC, and integrated photonic RC, with free-space conﬁgurations offering
the highest ﬂexibility and parallelism.
In this work, we propose and experimentally demonstrate an innovative free-space optical RC system for
forecasting daily stock index prices. Using a spatial light modulator (SLM), temporal benchmark data is projected
into high-dimensional optical signals across both spatial and temporal domains. This architecture enables high-
quality prediction while supporting ﬂexible encoding of multi-dimensional input features. Thanks to the large
number of modulator pixels and a novel feature-mapping method, the system is capable of processing multiple
training samples in parallel-regardless of input feature size-without incurring additional time costs for signal
interconnection or nonlinearity, as seen in traditional temporal RC systems [ 22].
By translating temporal dependencies in time-series data into spatial coherence within a free-space optical
platform, our system enables multi-step prediction with enhanced parallelism and speed. We evaluate performance
on seven widely-used stock indices, each with 500 historical data points and seven macroeconomic and technical
indicators. Feature correlation is incorporated as a weighting mechanism to further enhance prediction quality. Our
comparative analysis shows that the proposed optical RC system consistently outperforms popular ML models for
both one-step and multi-step prediction tasks. Unlike prior approaches that depend on large datasets, our system
achieves real-time, low-latency, and accurate forecasting with limited historical data. Notably, the integration of
feature-weighted mapping leads to immediate capture of signal peaks and improved robustness. These advantages
position our feature-optimized optical RC system as a promising solution for edge-based conﬁdential forecasting
and efﬁcient ﬁnancial modeling.

Optical RC
Our spatial optical RC follows the basic working principle of conventional RC as visualized in Fig. 2, which can
be described by
x(t) = f [αx(t − 1)Wres + βu(t)Win ], (1)
where x(t) represents the reservoir state at time t. f is the nonlinear function. α and β are the feedback gain and
input gain, which will be optimized for the best performance.Wres is the random connection of the reservoir nodes.
Win is the input weight to map the input to the system. In a conventional RC framework, the input vector x has a
dimension of L × 1, where L denotes the length of the time series. At each time step t, there is a single data point.
The input weight matrix Win is generated randomly with dimensions N × L,w h e r eN is the number of reservoir
nodes. This setup enables the projection of the input data into a higher-dimensional space. Due to the updating
rule of the reservoir states in Eq. 1, the current state x(t) inherently contains information from the preceding state
x(t − 1). This endows reservoir computers with the crucial property of short-term memory, a feature for effective
time-series data prediction. In the training stage, the input is injected sequentially into the reservoir, and the
resulting reservoir states x for all time instances within the range [1, T] are recorded. Subsequently, the system’s
output weights ( Wout ) can be determined by minimizing the error between the predicted results ( ˆy = Wout x).
In our optical setup, a continuous-wave 775 nm laser diode serves as the light source, as illustrated in Fig. 3.
The beam waist is adjusted to 1 mm after collimation. To match the effective area on the spatial light modulator
(Santec SLM-210, which is 15.4× 9.6 mm), a 10× beam expander is used to enlarge the beam size. A power meter
is implemented to capture the transmitted light passing through the polarizing beam splitter (PBS) for real-time
monitoring of system stability during training and testing. While the laser power is not directly incorporated into
the computational process, it is utilized as a diagnostic measure to mitigate the risk of erroneous results arising
from abrupt ﬂuctuations in the laser source. Before the beam splitter (BS), we employ a half-wave plate to rotate
the light’s polarization, thereby maximizing the modulation effect on the SLM. Following modulation, the light
is focused, and a camera (model: Basler a2A 1920-160umPRO with a 1920 × 1200 resolution) is positioned in
the time-Fourier domain [ 29] to align the sensor area with the beam size for subsequent information processing.
The inset in Fig. 3 illustrates the data ﬂow diagram in our spatial RC. One key here is the combination of
input values and the feedback state for spatial light modulation. Here, we use a similar mapping method as the
"linear-combination mapping" in our previous paper [ 21]. The difference is with how the overall mapping area is
split into several input portals. Instead of equally dividing the SLM into n sections for n input values, we repeat
and stack the inputs until all the modulation areas on the SLM are ﬁlled. In the case where n = 4, as shown in

Multiple ML and LSTM Models
This paper uses various ML regression methods and neural networks to predict each benchmark [ 30, 31]. These
include Lasso, ElasticNet, Random Forest Regressor, Linear Regression, Ridge, SGD Regressor, K-Nearest Neigh-
bors Regressor, Decision Tree Regressor, Bayesian Ridge, and LSTM [32–34]. Speciﬁcally, the LSTM architecture
includes an internal memory that acts like a local variable, storing information as it processes a sequence of inputs.
It uniquely handles outputs by recycling the production from one time-step as the input for the subsequent time-
step [35]. This recycling process enables the LSTM to make informed predictions by considering both the current
input and the output from the immediate preceding timestep. While LSTM is well-suited for time series prediction
due to its ability to capture temporal dependencies, it generally requires large datasets for effective training. To
ensure a fair comparison, we also evaluate several traditional machine learning regression models. We employ
cross-validation with time series splitting to rigorously evaluate and compare each model’s performance while
preserving the temporal structure of the data. After that, we apply grid search for hyperparameter tuning to optimize
the model’s conﬁguration, ensuring we identify the most effective parameters for the best-performing model. The
best-performing model will be selected for comparison with the optical RC system.
For multi-step prediction, the data preparation for the time-series sequence with features follows the same
approach as in optical RC in the previous subsection. The regression methods remain consistent with the one-step
prediction, while the model dimensions in the LSTM are adjusted to align with the prediction horizon.

In dynamic and highly non-stationary Financial environments especially like the stock market, concept drift
is a persistent issue: the underlying data-generating processes evolve over time due to changing market regimes,
investor behavior, and macroeconomic factors. As a result, models trained on large historical datasets often suffer
from degraded predictive performance when applied to more recent data, since the patterns they learn may no longer
be relevant [36]. The decision to train our models on relatively small datasets-speciﬁcally, 500 daily observations-
was made with careful consideration of the unique challenges posed by ﬁnancial time series. By focusing on
shorter, more recent time windows, we aim to mitigate the impact of outdated information and ensure that the
model reﬂects the current market context. The importance of handling concept drift in streaming or time-evolving
environments has been well documented in the literature [ 37], and is especially critical in ﬁnancial applications
where drift can occur both abruptly and incrementally.
Features and Correlation Weights
Due to the complexity of the benchmarks, a prediction model based solely on the closing price cannot gather all the
necessary information for accurate predictions. Consequently, feature selection is a crucial part of the prediction
enhancement. However, it is challenging to extract features from ﬁnancial data. On the one hand, limiting the
features can hinder the predictive model’s performance. On the other hand, including all available features from
the ﬁnancial market can lead to a dimensionally complex and difﬁcult-to-interpret model. Additionally, collinearity
among multiple variables can negatively impact the model’s performance. Some studies select the features rely
solely on technical indicators [ 38, 39], while others incorporate historical data and macroeconomic indicators
[40–44].
Table 2 outlines the set of input features used for stock prediction in this study, categorized into fundamental,
macroeconomic, and technical indicators. Fundamental data includes the current and lagged closing prices obtained
from Y ahoo Finance. Macroeconomic indicators incorporate market sentiment and economic conditions, such as
the Cboe V olatility Index (VIX), Effective Federal Funds Rate (EFFR), Consumer Sentiment Index (UMSCENT),
and the U.S. Dollar Index (DXYNYB), sourced from Y ahoo Finance and FRED. Technical analysis features,
including the Moving Average Convergence Divergence (MACD), Average True Range (A TR), and Relative
Strength Index (RSI), are calculated directly based on historical price data. These diverse features are designed to
capture both short-term dynamics and broader market conditions to enhance forecasting accuracy. The features are
selected by calculating the Pearson correlation coefﬁcients, which reﬂect the strength and direction of the linear
relationship between the features and the closing price of each stock index.

correlation matrix, as shown in Fig. 5. The numerical values in the heatmap represent the pairwise correlations
between the variables on the horizontal and vertical axes. As expected, the diagonal values of the matrix are all

effectiveness. All inputs are standardized using z-score normalization prior to training to ensure consistency in
scale. Further analysis and validation are presented in the Results and Discussion section.
4 EvaluationMetrics
Table 3 provides a comprehensive overview of the evaluation metrics used to assess the performance of the optical
RC and ML models in this study. The selected metrics span several categories to ensure a holistic evaluation.
Error-based metrics such as MAE, MSE, RMSE, NRMSE, and MAPE quantify the absolute and relative deviations
between predicted and actual values. The coefﬁcient of determination ( R2) captures the proportion of variance
explained by the model, while Directional Accuracy(%) measures the ability to predict the correct movement
direction.
To evaluate the performance of the proposed approach from a trading perspective, ﬁnancial performance
metrics—including Cumulative Return, Annualized Return, and Maximum Drawdown—are incorporated. Risk-
adjusted performance is evaluated using the Sharpe Ratio, Sortino Ratio, and Calmar Ratio, which account for
volatility and downside risk. Additional measures such as the Information Ratio, Win Rate, Proﬁt Factor, and Trade
Expectancy offer insight into proﬁtability, consistency, and expected outcomes of individual trades. Together, these
metrics provide a multidimensional framework for comparing model accuracy, robustness, and practical relevance
in ﬁnancial prediction tasks.

5 ResultsandDiscussion
In our experiments, we used 500 days of data for training and 100 days for testing. Among the ML baseline models,
Lasso performed best for 1-step prediction, while LSTM outperformed the other models for 4-step and 10-step
predictions. This is because Lasso’s L1 regularization helps prevent overﬁtting under limited data conditions.
In contrast, LSTM beneﬁts from its neural network architecture, which is well suited to capturing temporal
dependencies-particularly after data resampling during the 4-step and 10-step modeling. In the results that follow,
we compare only the best-performing ML models, labeled as Best ML, with the weighted optical RC system,
labeled as optical RC.
Figure 6 presents a subset of the experimental results, highlighting the signiﬁcant performance improvement
achieved by incorporating correlation weights into the optical RC system. Figures present 1-step prediction results
for the stock indices ˆNY A and ˆN225, respectively. For ˆNY A, the normalized root mean square errors (NRMSEs,
the formula is listed in Table3) of the RC model with and without Wcor are 0.104 and 0.108, respectively, while the
best ML model yields an NRMSE of 0.558-over ﬁve times higher than the RC result. Although the performance
difference between the optical RC systems with and without correlation weights is relatively small for ˆNY A, the
inclusion of Wcor still offers measurable improvement. In contrast, the effect of Wcor is much more signiﬁcant
for ˆN225, as shown in Fig. 6(b). The RC model without correlation weights yields an NRMSE of 0.688, while
the inclusion of Wcor reduces the error to 0.236-a substantial enhancement. This clearly demonstrates that the
feature correlation weights substantially boost the predictive power of the optical RC system, especially in more
complex market conditions. Furthermore, the optical RC system consistently outperforms the best ML model
across both benchmarks. For ˆN225, the top ML model results in an NRMSE of 1.309, signiﬁcantly worse than
both RC conﬁgurations. As visually evident in Fig. 6, the ML model predictions lag behind true price movements
and diverge from actual values, whereas the optical RC predictions more accurately capture market volatility and
nonlinear trends.
This indicates that the inclusion of correlation weights signiﬁcantly enhances the prediction accuracy of the
optical RC system by enabling it to better capture inter-feature relationships. The advantage of the weighted optical
RC is especially pronounced in Fig. 6b, where the optical RC with CW not only follows the trend more accurately
but also achieves better amplitude matching in the rising and falling phases of the ˆN225 index. In contrast, the best
ML model exhibits noticeable lag and under-performance, particularly during periods of sharp market movement.

6 Conclusion
In conclusion, we have established a weighted optical reservoir computing system in free space that effectively
captures broader market behavior and demonstrates superior performance in stock index prediction. Compared to
the existing machine learning methods and long short-term memory neural networks, our system ofﬂoads intense
computation to an optical system with low energy consumption, showing signiﬁcant advantages in handling high
volatility and nonlinear market behaviors using limited data. This method promises a cost-effective approach
to real-time, parallel, multi-dimensional data processing and predictions, with broad applications in ﬁnancial
forecasting. The next steps include moving more data processing into the optical domain for high-speed, high-
quality, and versatile applications in ﬁnancial analysis and alpha research. Future work could explore extending
RC architectures through hierarchical or multi-scale reservoirs to better capture long-term dependencies and
multi-scale temporal patterns in ﬁnancial time series analysis.
